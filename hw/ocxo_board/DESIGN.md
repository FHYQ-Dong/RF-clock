# Integrated OCXO board

This board lets node B replace its FS725 by an oven-controlled crystal oscillator (OCXO), steered through the OCXO's tuning voltage as the link now steers the FS725 through FC. It carries the OCXO and its supplies, a 20-bit DAC section that makes the tuning voltage, the SPI input through which node B's PL writes the DAC, and two 10 MHz outputs, one for the RFSoC 4x2 and one for a scope or White Rabbit during tests. The DAC section is the circuit of Analog Devices' EVAL-CN0531-PMDZ module, drawn onto the board.

An earlier design, plan 1 (2026-11-10), was a carrier board for two bought modules, the EVAL-CN0531-PMDZ as the DAC and an Adafruit FT232H through which the ARM wrote it over USB. It was given up on 2026-11-20 and is not in the repository. This board, plan 2, took over its OCXO, supplies, tuning filter and outputs with the same parts and names. The DAC moved onto the board, and the DAC's SPI now comes from the PL through an isolator, because the steering runs in the PL since rf-clock 2.5.

The KiCad project is generated by the scripts in `gen/`, and How the files are made (near the end) gives the order in which they run. The schematic is also exported as `ocxo_board.pdf`. The data sheets cited by page are the makers' current ones, found by part number. Those of the OCXO (NI-10M-3600), the AD5791 and the FS725, and the CN0531's design files, are kept in `hw/ref/`, which is not in Git, and the others (LT3471, LTC6655, ISO7741, NVT2008, TPS7A84A, the PRS10 inside the FS725, and the RFSoC 4x2's manual) are not kept.

## What it should give against the FS725

**What is steered.** Node B's clocks follow node A's over a radio link. The two nodes exchange bursts, one pair every 4 ms, and from each pair the link measures θ, the time of the 100 MHz clock in B's programmable logic (the PL) against the one in A's PL. Each node's clocks come from its own FS725, a rubidium frequency standard whose 10 MHz comes from a crystal oscillator that a frequency lock loop holds to the rubidium transition. A is the reference and is not steered. Node B's steering keeps θ at a fixed value θ₀ by changing FC, the FS725's setting that tunes its crystal, sent over RS-232. What the steering leaves is given as θ − θ₀, the rms of its one-second means. A frequency offset of 1×10⁻¹² is written 1 ps/s below, since a clock with that offset gains 1 ps each second. With this board the steering sets B's OCXO through the DAC instead.

**What the FS725 leaves.** Since rf-clock 2.5, node B's PL computes every step of the steering and sends each change to B's FS725 itself. With steps of 0.02 s it left θ − θ₀ at 0.59 and 0.64 ps on the bench on 2026-11-17 ([results/pl-best-20261117](../../results/pl-best-20261117/REPORT.md)). The FS725 follows a change of FC after a dead time, the time from the command until its crystal starts to move, and then with a lag of about 1 s, the time constant of the first-order response by which its frequency approaches the new value (both measured, that report's section 4.5). The dead time is 27.5 ms for a command sent right after its step's end, and it varies with when the command leaves, because the FS725 applies commands only at points about 14.5 ms apart (inferred there). The report infers that most of what is left comes from predicting the two clocks over that time. From replays of the steering on recorded clocks (next paragraph) it also infers that the link's measurement noise accounts for about 0.3 ps of the 0.55-0.65 ps that its best setting leaves, added in quadrature, which leaves 0.47-0.57 ps to the prediction.

**A faster actuator.** The same report replays the steering on recorded clocks with an idealised actuator in place of B's FS725 (its section 4.4). The actuator has a shorter dead time or lag than the FS725, the steering's model of it is exact, its tuning is not rounded and its range is not limited, while the records keep both FS725s' noise and the link's measurement noise. With steps of 0.02 s and a dead time of 1 ms, which stands for a DAC that the PL writes directly, it left 0.18-0.20 ps on the two records with a lag of 0.01 s and 0.27-0.28 ps with a lag of 0.1 s. With the FS725 the measurement noise alone accounted for about 0.3 ps, more than these actuators' whole 0.18-0.20 ps. So with them the same noise reaches θ − θ₀ less, by an amount the report did not measure.

**This board.** Here the dead time is short. A write of 24 bits takes 24 µs at an SCLK of 1 MHz, and the DAC's output settles at the OCXO's tuning pin within about 0.5 ms (see Simulations). The lag is the OCXO's own, how fast its frequency follows its tuning voltage, which its data sheet does not give. If that lag is 0.1 s or shorter, this board may bring θ − θ₀ to about 0.2-0.3 ps. That is an order of magnitude and not a forecast, because the replays keep B's FS725's noise. The OCXO replaces it with its own, while A keeps its FS725. At an averaging time of 1 s the OCXO is quieter than the FS725. Its data sheet gives a limit of 5×10⁻¹² for the Allan deviation at 1 s (p. 1), and for one tested unit 1.5×10⁻¹² at 1 s and about 1.1×10⁻¹² at 0.1 s (p. 3). Between two free-running FS725s, θ's time deviation at 1 s was 8.2 ps ([FC response report](../../results/fc-response-20261112/REPORT.md), section 9), so θ's modified Allan deviation at 1 s is √3 × 8.2 ps / 1 s = 1.4×10⁻¹¹, and each FS725's about 1.0×10⁻¹¹ if the two are alike. For the usual kinds of oscillator noise the plain Allan deviation is not smaller than the modified one, so this comparison favours the FS725, if anything. But at the averaging times of 0.02-0.1 s that matter for steps of 0.02 s, the tested unit's Allan deviation, after falling from 1 s to 0.1 s, rises again, to about 2-3×10⁻¹² at 20-30 ms (p. 3), and the FS725's was not measured there. An earlier estimate in section 5 of [foresight.md](../../results/fc-response-20261112/foresight.md), written when the ARM was still to write the DAC and before these replays, gave 0.29-0.36 ps for steps of 20 ms computed in the PL. It assumed that the OCXO follows its tuning with no lag, scaled the FS725's noise to the OCXO's at 1 s, and rested on the prediction's error less than 0.1 s ahead, extrapolated from 0.1-0.2 s, so it called the figure only an order. It lies just above the replays' 0.18-0.28 ps. The OCXO's lag is the first thing to measure once the board works.

**What it costs.** Without the FS725, node B has no rubidium of its own. If the link drops, B's time drifts with the OCXO. At the data sheet's aging limit of 5×10⁻¹¹ a day (p. 2, Table 1, after 30 days of running), the time error grows as half the drift rate times the time squared, ½ × (5×10⁻¹¹ / 86 400 s) × (86 400 s)² ≈ 2 µs after a day, against about 0.07 µs for the FS725's rubidium oscillator, the PRS10, which ages at most 5×10⁻¹¹ a month (PRS10 manual, p. 4). These are the aging parts alone. A frequency error left when the link drops adds to them, about 0.9 µs in a day for an error of 1×10⁻¹¹, an example. The OCXO's temperature dependence, up to ±0.1 ppb over −10 to 70 °C (p. 2, Table 1), adds more if the board's temperature changes. The board is meant to sit in a closed box that slows its temperature changes, called the thermal box below, which is not designed yet.

## The OCXO

The OCXO is the Taitien NI-10M-3660, a double-oven OCXO in a 36.3 × 27.2 mm DIP package (data sheet `hw/ref/NI-10M-3600.pdf`, XO-0238 Rev 3). It holds ±0.1 ppb from −10 to 70 °C and ages less than 0.05 ppb a day after 30 days. If the board is ever used outdoors at −40 to 85 °C, as the planned 48 km air link across Long Island Sound (milestone M6, [docs/technical_report.md](../../docs/technical_report.md) section 8.2) may need, the NI-10M-3610 of the same series fits the same footprint.

| Data sheet item | Value | What it means for the board |
|---|---|---|
| Supply | 4.75 to 5.25 V, 2.5 W steady at 25 °C, 1.75 A during warm-up | the 5 V regulator carries 1.75 A, the adapter about 1 A at 12 V |
| Supply sensitivity | ±0.1 ppb for a ±5 % supply change | about 4×10⁻¹⁰ per volt, so the 5 V comes from a low-noise linear regulator, not straight from an adapter |
| Output | CMOS, "1" at least 2.4 V, "0" at most 0.3 V, 15 pF load | drives one buffer input close by and nothing else |
| Tuning (Vcon) | at least ±0.15 ppm over 0 to 5 V, center 2.5 V, positive slope, input at least 50 kΩ, held at the center inside when open | the slope is at least 6×10⁻⁸ per volt, so 1×10⁻¹² is about 17 µV |
| Pins (bottom view) | 1 Vcon bottom left, 2 not connected middle left, 3 +VDC top left, 4 output top right, 5 0 V and case bottom right | pins 25.40 mm apart along the long side and 17.80 mm along the short side, 0.79 mm diameter |

The tuning range must also cover the unit's own offset. At 2.5 V a unit may be off by up to ±0.1 ppm within 90 days of its date code (p. 1, initial accuracy), which leaves at least 0.05 ppm of the ±0.15 ppm on the side with less room, and aging of up to 10 ppb a year (p. 2, Table 1) eats into that, in the worst case to 0.03 ppm after two years. 0.03 ppm is 30 000 ps/s, while a correction of 1.5 ps within 0.1 s needs about 15 ps/s, so the range is ample, and the unit's real offset is measured when it arrives.

The footprint is drawn from the outline drawing on page 4 of the data sheet, mirrored top to bottom, because the drawing is a bottom view. Pin 1 then lies at the top left of the top view, next to the marking dot, as the table's pin list turned over gives. The footprint is still to be checked against the part (see To check before ordering).

## The supplies

The board runs from one 12 V adapter. The adapter's output must float, with no connection to mains earth, so that the board's ground reaches other equipment only through the coax shields (see Grounding). It must give at least 2 A.

The input goes through a 2 A resettable fuse, an SS34 diode against reverse polarity and an SMAJ15A that clamps surges. A TPS563208 buck converter then makes 5.64 V. This part switches at a fixed 580 kHz at every load, so its ripple stays at one known frequency. A linear regulator alone could not do this step, because it would have to burn about 12 W while the OCXO warms up. After the buck a ferrite bead and 22 µF keep its ripple off the rail that feeds the three linear regulators.

| Rail | Regulator | Load | Why this part |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5.0 V for the OCXO | U2, TPS7A8400A, set by resistors to 4.985 V | 1.75 A during warm-up, 0.5 A steady | 3 A, 4.4 µV rms noise and high rejection of the buck's ripple. It drops 0.65 V and dissipates 1.1 W during warm-up and 0.3 W afterwards on its thermal pad |
| +3V3_DAC for the DAC section and the board's half of the isolator U13 | U3, LP38693MP-3.3 in SOT-223 | about 0.1 A (inferred, the DAC section draws about 17 mA from +7.5 V and 11 mA from −7.5 V by the data sheets' typical currents, about 0.22 W at ±8 V, which the LT3471 makes from 3.3 V at an assumed 70 % efficiency) | 0.5 A, and it dissipates about (5.64 − 3.3) V × 0.1 A ≈ 0.23 W, which the SOT-223's tab spreads into the copper. A SOT-23 regulator would heat up by about 70 °C here (inferred, at about 300 °C/W) |
| +3V3_CLK for the clock buffer | U4, TPS7A2033 | about 20 mA | low noise, and a rail of its own so the buffer's switching currents do not reach the DAC's supply |

U2's power-good output is on a test point, and the 5 V and +3V3_DAC each light an LED (D3 and D5).

## The DAC section

The circuit is copied from the CN0531 schematic (02_058962 Rev B, sheet 2, in `hw/ref/CN0531-DesignSupport/`) with its jumpers set for a 0-5 V output, and keeps the CN0531's values. Each step feeds the next.

1. **±8 V.** The LT3471 switches at 1.2 MHz and makes +8 V with a boost converter (L2, D6) and −8 V with an inverter (L3, C46, D7, L4) from +3V3_DAC. R32 and R33 (105 kΩ and 15 kΩ) set +8 V, and R34 and R35 (15 kΩ and 120 kΩ) set −8 V, if the LT3471's feedback and reference voltages are 1.000 V as on the CN0531, which is still to be checked in its data sheet. R30 with C40 and R31 with C41 make both outputs start slowly.
2. **±7.5 V.** The LT3042 makes +7.5 V from +8 V and the LT3093 makes −7.5 V from −8 V. Each sets its output with 75 kΩ at its SET pin, which carries 100 µA, and 4.7 µF on that pin filters the regulator's own reference noise. These two regulators remove the LT3471's 1.2 MHz ripple before it reaches the reference and the amplifiers. Their power-good outputs only have their pull-up resistors. The CN0531 lights an LED from them through an NC7SZ11 gate, which this board leaves out.
3. **The 5 V reference.** The LTC6655-5 makes 5.000 V (REF5) from +7.5 V. It drifts by at most 2 ppm per °C, which is 5 µV per °C at the 2.5 V center and about 3×10⁻¹³ per °C at the smallest tuning slope, so the board sits in a thermal box. Its noise from 0.1 to 10 Hz is 0.25 ppm of its output peak to peak, 1.25 µV at 5 V, which reaches DAC_OUT at the 2.5 V center as about 0.6 µV, or 3.7×10⁻¹⁴ at the smallest tuning slope.
4. **The reference buffers.** One half of the AD8676 drives the AD5791's positive reference input from REF5, and the other half holds its negative reference input at ground. Each reference input has a force pin and a sense pin, and the amplifier's feedback comes from the sense pin, so the voltage drop in the force pin's track and bond wire is taken out. This is the CN0531 with its jumper P10 fitted (negative reference at ground).
5. **The DAC.** The AD5791 runs from +7.5 V and −7.5 V, and its logic from +3V3_DAC. A BAT54 from +3V3_DAC to +7.5 V keeps the analog supply from falling more than the Schottky diode's drop, about 0.3 V, below the logic supply while the supplies come up, as on the CN0531.
6. **The output buffer.** The AD8675 buffers the DAC's output. The AD5791's feedback resistor RFB and its INV pin are tied to the buffer's output and inverting input (the CN0531's jumper P13), with 10 pF across them. With the RBUF bit of the AD5791's control register set to 1, the buffer runs at unity gain and DAC_OUT spans 0 to 5 V, the OCXO's whole tuning range. One step of the DAC is 4.8 µV, about 2.9×10⁻¹³ at the smallest slope the data sheet allows.

The parts differ from the CN0531's in these places, all with the same values.

## The tuning filter

DAC_OUT goes through these parts in order to the OCXO's tuning pin.

1. **R8.** A 100 Ω thin-film resistor (0.1 %, 25 ppm per °C) is in series. It keeps the 1.1 µF below from loading the AD8675, which could make it oscillate, and together with the capacitors it is a low-pass filter with a corner at 1.45 kHz (1/(2π × 100 Ω × 1.1 µF)). The filter removes the LT3471's 1.2 MHz ripple and anything picked up on the way. Its time constant is 0.11 ms, far shorter than the 0.02 s between corrections. The OCXO's tuning input is at least 50 kΩ to its own 2.5 V center, so it draws at most 50 µA through the resistor, and the resistor lowers the voltage by at most 5 mV, a fixed share of the deviation from 2.5 V that the steering takes out like any other gain error. Its temperature coefficient moves that by about 0.1 µV per °C.
2. **C21 and C22.** 1 µF and 100 nF go from Vcon to ground, both C0G. Class 2 ceramics such as X7R change capacity with voltage, leak, and turn vibration into voltage, so they are not used on this node.
3. **D4.** A BAV199 clamps Vcon, with one diode to ground and one to the OCXO's 5 V. It leaks a few pA, which makes no measurable drop across 100 Ω. A Schottky clamp would leak about 1 µA, which would be 100 µV, about 6×10⁻¹², and would change with temperature. If the control register is ever left with RBUF at 0, DAC_OUT spans −5 to +5 V, and the clamp holds Vcon between about −0.6 V and 5.6 V, a diode's drop beyond the OCXO's ground and supply.
4. **TP6.** A test point sits on Vcon.

## The SPI input from the RFSoC 4x2

Node B's PL writes the AD5791 over SPI. The lines leave the RFSoC 4x2 at PMOD0, one of the two Pmod connectors in its 2 × 15 right-angle socket J1. That J1 is the 4x2's, not this board's 12 V jack J1, so it is called the 4x2's J1 below. A 2×6 Pmod extension cable takes the lines to J8, a right-angle 2×6 male header on this board's bottom edge, and they pass the digital isolator U13, an ISO7741, on their way to the AD5791.

```
 node B's PL ─► NVT2008 (4x2) ─► 4x2's J1, PMOD0 ─► Pmod cable ─► J8 ─► U13 side 1 │ U13 side 2 ─► AD5791
 3.3 V and ground of the 4x2 ─────────────► J8 pins 12 and 6, 11 and 5 ─► U13 side 1 │ +3V3_DAC and the board's ground ─► U13 side 2
                                                                                 │
                              island of the 4x2's ground (all four layers)       │  the board's ground
```

**Why the lines are isolated.** This board's ground meets the 4x2's ground at one point only, the shield of the 10 MHz coax to the 4x2 (see Grounding). The cable's ground pins would join the two grounds a second time, and currents from the 4x2 would then flow in this board's ground and add their voltage to the tuning voltage. U13 passes logic levels across an insulating barrier, with no conducting path. The ISO7741 data sheet calls its two halves side 1 and side 2. Side 1, pins 1 to 8, faces the cable. It runs from the 4x2's 3.3 V and ground, which come through the cable, and is joined to nothing else on the board. Side 2, pins 9 to 16, faces the DAC and runs from +3V3_DAC and this board's ground. C73 (100 nF) and C74 (10 µF) decouple side 1's supply, and C75 (100 nF) side 2's. Side 1's copper lies on an island, a separate area of copper that carries the 4x2's ground on all four layers, and 2 mm of bare board separate it from this board's ground on every layer (see Layout).

**Pins.** J8 is wired in Pmod numbering, pins 1 to 6 in the upper row and 7 to 12 in the lower row. The 4x2's line names and FPGA pins are those of the 4x2's pin list (the board support package's 4x2_PMOD.xdc).

| J8 pin | 4x2 line (FPGA pin) | Signal | Direction | Path through U13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 | PMOD0_4 (AF15) | SDIN, data to the DAC | 4x2 to board | channel A, pin 3 to pin 14 |
| 8 | PMOD0_5 (AF17) | SCLK, the clock | 4x2 to board | channel B, pin 4 to pin 13 |
| 9 | PMOD0_6 (AH17) | SYNC, low for the length of a frame | 4x2 to board | channel C, pin 5 to pin 12 |
| 10 | PMOD0_7 (AK16) | SDO, data read back from the DAC | board to 4x2 | channel D, pin 11 to pin 6, then R10 (33 Ω) |
| 11 and 5 | ground | the 4x2's ground | | pins 2 and 8, side 1's ground |
| 12 and 6 | 3.3 V | the 4x2's 3.3 V | 4x2 to board | pin 1, side 1's supply, and pin 7, EN1, which enables side 1's output (channel D) |
| 1 | PMOD0_0 (AF16) | the output PPS's validity line | 4x2 to board | not connected |
| 2 to 4 | PMOD0_1 to PMOD0_3 | | | not connected |

The SPI uses only the lower row's signal pins, while the supply pins of both rows are connected. Pin 1 carries the output PPS's validity line, which is high while the output PPS is not valid ([plan/pps_output.md](../../plan/pps_output.md)), and pins 2 to 4 stay free for other uses. The cable carries the validity line on to J8's pin 1, which this board leaves unconnected, so with the cable plugged in the line is not reachable for a probe. The 4x2's LED W_LED_0 (silkscreen PL_LED0) still shows it, lit while the output PPS is valid. On side 2, EN2 (pin 10) is tied to +3V3_DAC, which enables side 2's outputs, channels A to C.

Digilent's SPI order puts SYNC (chip select) on pin 1, SDIN on 2, SDO on 3 and SCLK on 4 of the upper row, which would be 7, 8, 9 and 10 in the lower row. J8 differs from it so that the tracks follow the order in which the pins lie on the board. No two tracks cross between J8 and U13, and between U13 and the AD5791 only SDO crosses the other three, on the bottom layer. On the 4x2's J1, Pmod pin 7 is the hole at the end of the lower row at the PMOD0 end, below pin 1, and pins 8 to 10 follow towards the middle. The 4x2's pin list gives only names and FPGA pins, so that pins 7 to 10 are PMOD0_4 to PMOD0_7 follows Digilent's usual numbering. On the same socket the bench showed, for PMOD1's lower row, that the FS725's replies arrive on PMOD1_7 at its Pmod pin 10 and that PMOD1_6 drives pin 9 ([docs/ABI.md](../../docs/ABI.md), the section on the PL's serial line, 2026-11-16), which agrees with that numbering for two of the four pins. PMOD0's has not been checked. A wrong mapping is fixed in the FPGA's pin constraints, not on this board. To check it, the PL block that writes the DAC (P14, below) has a test signal that the ARM switches on and off. Every 20 µs it raises SDIN once, SCLK twice and pulls SYNC low three times, each for 1 µs, and never pulls SYNC low while SCLK pulses, so the three lines can be told apart by counting pulses on a scope at the 4x2's J1, and an AD5791 connected by mistake takes no frame. P14 also counts the falling edges on SDO, so a jumper from J1's pin for SDIN to the pin for SDO shows SDO's pin as well. The check runs with the cable unplugged, so that the scope probes the 4x2's J1 directly. SDO's pin stays an input throughout, since on the board U13's channel D drives that line through R10.

**What the PL has to do.** The interface section of the AD5791 data sheet (Rev. F, pages 19 to 24, in `hw/ref/ad5791.pdf`) gives the details. These are the parts that this board fixes or that the PL needs.

- LDAC is held low and RESET and CLR high by the board's 10 kΩ resistors, so the PL drives only SYNC, SCLK and SDIN, and reads SDO only for a readback. With LDAC low the output takes a new code at the rising edge of SYNC that ends its frame.
- A frame is 24 bits, most significant bit first. Bit 23 is R/W (0 to write), bits 22 to 20 the register address (001 the DAC register, 010 the control register) and bits 19 to 0 the data (Tables 7 and 8, p. 19). SYNC goes low, the AD5791 takes SDIN at each falling edge of SCLK, and SYNC goes high after the 24th falling edge. So SCLK can rest low between frames with SDIN changing after each rising edge (SPI mode 1). The minimum times in Table 4 (p. 5) are at most tens of ns, far below the 500 ns half period of a 1 MHz SCLK.
- After power-up the AD5791 holds its output at ground through about 6 kΩ, with its DAC switched off (the OPGND and DACTRI bits of the control register set, Tables 11 and 12, p. 22), so the OCXO starts at the low end of its range. One write to the control register clears both and keeps RBUF set, which puts the output buffer at unity gain for 0-5 V. That word is 0x200012 for offset binary coding (BIN/2sC = 1), where code 0 gives 0 V and code 0x80000 gives 2.5 V, the word the PL design uses. The steering then writes frames 0x1nnnnn to the DAC register. One code step is 5 V / 2²⁰ = 4.8 µV, about 0.29 ps/s of the OCXO's frequency for its tuning range of ±0.15 ppm over 0-5 V.
- A readback is two frames. The first has R/W = 1 and the address, 0xA00000 for the control register, and the second is a NOP (all zeros), during which the AD5791 shifts the register out on SDO and changes SDO at most 12 ns after each rising edge of SCLK (t19, Table 4, for a logic supply of 1.71-3.3 V). SCLK reaches the board through the translator and U13, and SDO comes back through U13, R10, the cable and the translator. U13 takes 13.5 ns each way (typical, at 3.3 V, ISO7741 data sheet SLLSEP4K, whose maximum was not looked up), and SCLK's rising edge is slow on the 4x2's side (next paragraph), so SDO is back at the PL within about 200 ns of SCLK's rising edge there (inferred). At 1 MHz the PL samples it at the next falling edge, 500 ns after the rising one.
- At start-up, and again whenever the board may have lost power, the control word goes first and the code to start from second. The software of the PL design (below) decides that code. A new unit would start from 0x80000 (2.5 V), and later ones from the code last used. Until the first write the OCXO sits at the low end of its range, at least 0.15 ppm below its frequency at 2.5 V. The unit's code-to-frequency slope, measured when it arrives (see To check on the bench), goes into the steering's model.
- If this board loses power while the 4x2 runs, the AD5791 comes back in its power-up state, and the OCXO's tuning voltage goes to 0 V, the bottom of its range. The PL cannot see that directly. So the ARM reads the control register back now and then through P14, which lets it send a frame and read the reply, and writes the control word and the current code again when it finds OPGND set.

On the 4x2 every Pmod line passes an NVT2008 level translator between the FPGA's 1.8 V bank (LVCMOS18) and the socket, with 1.15 kΩ pull-ups to 3.3 V on the socket's side (the 4x2's manual p. 15 and schematic sheet 5, as plan/pps_output.md cites). A falling edge is driven through the translator, but a rising edge is the pull-up charging the line. With 20-60 pF of cable and inputs (inferred for a cable of about 15 cm), the time constant is 23-69 ns and the rise from 10 to 90 % takes 50-150 ns. SCLK should therefore stay at about 1 MHz or below. PL/constraints/rf_clock_4x2.xdc assigns the four lines as dac_sdin, dac_sclk, dac_sync_n and dac_sdo to the FPGA pins in the table, as LVCMOS18, with the FPGA's pull-up on dac_sdo, as for the other inputs that come through the same translators. While the 4x2 is off, while it loads its bitstream, or while the cable is unplugged, the pull-ups or U13 keep the lines high. U13 is the ISO7741, whose outputs stay high when side 1 has no power (the ISO7741F would hold them low). SYNC then stays high and the DAC keeps its code.

R10 sits in series with the one line this board drives into the cable, SDO, to damp reflections on it. The lines from the 4x2 have no series resistor. Their falling edges are driven fast through the translator's pass transistor, whose few ohms (inferred) do not damp a 15 cm cable, so they may ring (inferred). U13's inputs have hysteresis (ISO7741 data sheet), and the bench check looks for ringing on SCLK's falling edge, the edge on which the AD5791 samples. SYNC's 10 kΩ pull-up (R24) holds SYNC high if U13 is not fitted, for example while the DAC section is brought up without it, and with U13 fitted it changes nothing.

**The PL block and the cable.** In the PL design of rf-clock 2.6, in progress on 2026-11-20, the steering block P13 puts out a 20-bit DAC code at each 0.02 s steering step instead of an FC command, and a new block, P14, writes it to the AD5791. Its registers will be described in docs/ABI.md. The cable needs a male 2×6 plug for the 4x2's socket and a female 2×6 socket for J8, wired pin to pin, and as short as the setup allows, about 15 cm, which the rise-time estimate above assumes. It plugs into PMOD0's end of the 4x2's J1, the end opposite the one the 4x2's silkscreen marks PMODB (PMOD1's), the way a Pmod module would, and onto J8 with its pin 1 on the pin marked 1. As long as node B keeps its FS725 while this board is brought up and compared with it, the MAX3232 RS-232 module through which the PL talks to the FS725 stays in PMOD1's lower row at the other end of the 4x2's J1 ([docs/ABI.md](../../docs/ABI.md)), with three positions of the socket between the two.

## The 10 MHz outputs

The OCXO's output goes through a 0 Ω resistor (R17) to both inputs of a 74LVC2G34 (U5), a pair of buffers on +3V3_CLK. Its inputs accept up to 5.5 V at a 3.3 V supply, so the OCXO's CMOS output drives them directly, and the OCXO sees only a few pF instead of a cable. The 0 Ω resistor is a place for a series resistor if the OCXO's edges ring.

Each buffer output goes through 33 Ω and 100 nF to an edge-mounted SMA. The buffer's own output resistance is about 15 to 30 Ω, so the source is close to 50 Ω and a reflection from a mismatched load is absorbed at the source. The 100 nF blocks DC. Without it, a load terminated in 50 Ω to ground, such as a scope input set to 50 Ω, would draw about 34 mA while the output is high, more than the 24 mA the buffer is specified for. With it, the current swings about ±16 mA.

J6 goes to the 4x2's CLK IN (J14), which has its own 100 nF and a 3 dB attenuator before a 50 Ω input. In the second simulation below the LMK04828 there sees 1.14 V peak to peak, with an edge of 0.38 to 1.6 V/ns, at least 2.5 times the 0.15 V/ns it needs.

J7 must also end in 50 Ω, a scope input set to 50 Ω for example. Into a high-impedance input the cable is terminated only at the source, so at each edge the buffer charges the cable with 34 to 42 mA for about 10 ns, the time the edge takes to reach the far end and come back. That is more than the 24 mA the buffer is specified for. Into 50 Ω it stays at or below 22 mA. The silkscreen marks J6 "4x2 CLK IN" and J7 "50R ONLY".

## Grounding

The board has one ground, not split into separate analog and digital grounds, apart from the island of the 4x2's ground at the SPI input. The buck converter and its input run along the top edge, the OCXO sits in the middle, the DAC section to its right and the tuning filter below its tuning pin. The board's ground must meet the 4x2's ground at one point only, the shield of the 10 MHz coax on J6. That is why the SPI input is isolated and the adapter's output floats. If the Pmod cable or the adapter also joined the two grounds, currents from the 4x2 would flow in the board's ground and add their voltage to Vcon.

The second output, J7, is for tests. It closes another ground loop through the scope or White Rabbit when a cable is connected to it, which is acceptable then. In steady operation it is left open or fitted with a DC block that breaks both the inner and the outer conductor.

## Mechanics

The board is 115 × 100 mm with four plain M3 holes, without copper, their centres 3.5 mm from each edge at the corners, for a base plate inside the thermal box. They carry no ground, so a metal base plate cannot join the board's ground a second time. The two SMA connectors clip over the left edge and J8's pins stick out 6 mm over the bottom edge, so the base plate leaves room for both cables. Only the 12 V lead, the coax and the Pmod cable leave the board.

## Layout

The layout is generated by `gen/gen_pcb.py` (run with KiCad's Python, with Freerouting's command-line version freerouting-cli on PATH) and checked with KiCad's design rule check, which also compares the board with the schematic. The finished board has no DRC error, no unconnected item and no difference from the schematic (KiCad's report `build/drc.json`, which the scripts write again and which is not kept in Git).

**The copper layers.** The board is a stack of four thin copper layers with insulation between them, and each copper layer is etched into the shapes the circuit needs. Seen from the side, the stack is as follows, not to scale. With no stackup specified in the order, JLCPCB builds a four-layer 1.6 mm board in its standard stackup, which puts about 0.2 mm of 7628 prepreg (glass-fibre epoxy) between each outer layer and its inner plane, and the thicknesses below are that stackup's. KiCad's names for the layers are on the left.

```
 F.Cu    top layer      tracks and pads, with ground fill around them      35 µm copper
         insulation     glass-fibre epoxy, about 0.2 mm
 In1.Cu  inner layer 1  ground plane                                       about 15 µm copper
         insulation     glass-fibre epoxy, about 1.07 mm
 In2.Cu  inner layer 2  ground plane                                       about 15 µm copper
         insulation     glass-fibre epoxy, about 0.2 mm
 B.Cu    bottom layer   tracks and pads, with ground fill around them      35 µm copper
```

Together the layers are about 1.6 mm thick. The inner copper is ordered as 0.5 oz, 17.5 µm, and ends up about 15 µm thick after processing. This section uses a few words for what lies on these layers.

- **Track.** A track is a strip of copper on one layer that carries one connection, the board's wire.
- **Pad.** A pad is the copper a part's pin is soldered to. A surface-mount (SMD) part sits on pads on the top layer only. A through-hole part has pins that go through plated holes in the board, so its pads are on every layer.
- **Via.** A via is a small hole plated with copper that joins a track or pad on one layer to copper on the others.
- **Net.** A net is all the copper that the schematic connects together, for example ground (GND) or the tuning voltage. One net is one conductor, however many layers it spans.
- **Ground plane and ground fill.** A ground plane is a layer that carries only ground, here the two inner layers. Ground fill is ground copper filled in around the tracks of a layer that also carries tracks, here the two outer layers. Where a track runs through the fill, the track and the 0.3 mm gap on each side of it cut a slot into the fill, a strip without ground copper.
- **Stitching vias.** Stitching vias are ground vias spread over the board, one every 5 mm where there is room, that join the ground of all four layers into one.

**Why four layers.** The board has 1416 mm of track on the top layer and 594 mm on the bottom layer, both without the short tracks from ground pads to their vias, and more than half of the bottom layer's length, 315 mm, lies around the DAC, more than 78 mm from the left edge and 38 mm from the top. On the bottom layer (bottom right panel) the ground fill is still one piece, covering 90 % of the board's area. On the top layer (top left) the largest piece covers 71 %, and the tracks cut off 12 smaller ones. The two largest of these are a strip of 726 mm² along the bottom, between the +7.5 V track 80 mm from the top edge and the +3V3_DAC track 95 mm from it, and a piece of 152 mm² in the DAC section, 83 to 95 mm from the left edge and 42 to 65 mm from the top. Each is joined to the rest of the ground only through its vias to the inner planes. On a two-layer board with the tracks where they are, those pieces could reach the rest of the ground only through vias to the bottom layer's fill, which the bottom layer's own tracks slot as well (inferred, a two-layer version was not routed, and its tracks would lie somewhat differently). Four layers were chosen for five reasons.

1. **A voltage along the ground adds to the tuning voltage.** The OCXO sees the DAC's output voltage, which the AD8675 makes against the ground under the DAC section, less the voltage between that ground and the OCXO's own ground pin. 17 µV of the latter already changes the OCXO's frequency by 1 ps/s (a fraction 1×10⁻¹² of its 10 MHz, B's clock gaining 1 ps each second) at the smallest tuning slope the data sheet allows, and by more on a unit with a steeper slope. Currents flow in that ground on their way back to where they came from (their return currents). The OCXO's supply current (0.5 A steady, 1.75 A during warm-up) flows back to U2 and the buck converter, and the buck converter's current flows back to the 12 V jack. On two layers the ground is only the ground fill of the two outer layers, slotted wherever tracks run, like that of the top layer in the figure. Where slots lie across a current's path, the current has to go around them or through the narrow strip of copper left between them. As an example, copper 35 µm thick (1 oz, the usual outer layer) has 0.49 mΩ between opposite edges of a square, whatever the square's size, so a strip 2 mm wide and 10 mm long, five squares in a row, has 2.5 mΩ. If the OCXO's current changed by 10 mA (2 % of 0.5 A, an example, not a figure from the data sheet) and that change passed through such a strip between the DAC section and the OCXO's ground pin, the tuning voltage would move by 25 µV, which is at least 1.5 ps/s. The two inner planes have no such slots. Each is 0.5 oz copper (about 15 µm thick), so together they have 0.57 mΩ per square, and the same 10 mA spreading out from the OCXO's ground pin makes about 3 µV between points 1 mm and 30 mm from it ($V = \frac{I R_\square}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}$ for an uncut sheet with sheet resistance $R_\square$). A change as slow as the oven's current, which follows the box's temperature over minutes, the steering takes out like any drift of the oscillator. What it would not take out is a ground voltage that changes within a few steps, faster than the steering follows, and slower than about 1.45 kHz, the corner of the filter at the tuning pin. How large that part would be on two layers was not estimated, and the planes keep it small without that estimate. These figures are estimates, not measurements.
2. **The layout is routed by a program.** On two layers, where Freerouting puts the tracks decides where the ground is cut, so someone would have to check after every routing run where each return current flows. The inner planes have only round holes, around the 56 vias of other nets and the pins of the through-hole parts, wherever the tracks go, and one cut that does not depend on the routing, the 2 mm gap around the island.
3. **Fast currents flow back close to their tracks.** A slow current spreads over a plane along the paths of least resistance, but a fast one, such as the current of the buck converter's switching edges or of each 10 MHz edge, flows back right under its own track, where the loop it encloses has the least inductance. Here that plane lies about 0.2 mm under the top layer, instead of on the far side of a two-layer board 1.5 mm away. The loop's area is the track's length times its height above the plane, so it is about seven times smaller, and the voltage the loop's magnetic field induces in a nearby track, such as the tuning voltage's, falls by about that factor or more (inferred).
4. **The DAC section's tracks need the planes.** The pieces of top-layer ground fill described above reach the rest of the ground only through the inner planes.
5. **The reference lines run under a plane.** The AD5791's reference lines run on the bottom layer with a whole ground plane about 0.2 mm above them. On the top layer, in the first routing runs of this board, they enclosed pins of the AD5791 that the router could then not reach. On a two-layer board they would cross top-layer tracks with no ground plane between them, only the 1.5 mm of board. Where two tracks cross, the small capacitance between them couples fast edges on one, such as those of the DAC's SPI lines, into the other, and a ground plane between them takes that coupling to ground instead (inferred).

The inner planes also draw heat from U2 through the vias in its thermal pad (1.1 W during warm-up). How much that lowers U2's temperature was not computed.

**What the script adds to plain routing.**

- The AD5791's reference lines, force and sense for each of its two reference inputs, are fixed tracks drawn before routing. Each leaves its pin through a short track to a via and runs on the bottom layer straight down from the AD8676 to the AD5791, about 20 mm, past the output buffer. On the top layer these four lines would enclose the AD5791's pins between its two reference inputs, which go to the output buffer, and the router then left some connections open. On the bottom layer they cross the other tracks with the two ground planes between them, which take the coupling between the tracks to ground (inferred).
- Two grounded pins between signal pins at 0.5 mm pitch, the LT3471's FB2N (pin 5, a feedback input tied to ground) and the LT3042's ground pin 8, are joined to their chips' exposed pads by short tracks, and each of those exposed pads has two vias with 0.2 mm holes to the ground planes. A ground pin between pins at 0.65 mm pitch, such as the AD5791's pin 19, gets a 0.25 mm track to its via where a 0.4 mm one does not fit.
- The SPI input from the 4x2 sits on an island at the bottom right, 18 mm wide and 16.5 mm high, where all four layers carry the 4x2's ground instead of the board's. It holds J8, U13's side 1, C73, C74 and R10. The 2 mm gap around it runs under U13's body between its two rows of pins, so U13 is the only part that bridges it. The script keeps every other net out of the island and its gap, and the 4x2's nets inside the island. Freerouting does not know about the island, so once it has routed, the script takes up every track on the wrong side and lets the grid router join that net again on its own side. On this board that was one +3V3_DAC track that Freerouting had laid through the island, and the grid router then reached U13 and C75 with a 0.25 mm track from the left, ample for the few mA that U13's side 2 draws (ISO7741 data sheet, inferred). Every ground pin gets its via on its own ground's side.
- The grid router joined one branch of the 5.6 V rail, the one to U4 (about 20 mA), with a 0.4 mm track instead of 0.8 mm, which is ample for that current.

**Where the parts are.** The 12 V input and the buck converter run along the top edge, with the OCXO's 5 V regulator U2 below the buck converter, and U3 at the top right. The OCXO sits in the middle. The LT3471 and its inductors are at the left, 60 to 80 mm from the reference and the DAC, so that the 1.2 MHz currents of the switching converter flow in the ground planes far from them. The LT3042 and LT3093 are along the bottom edge at the left. To the right of the OCXO, from the top, are the reference U9 beside its buffers U10, then the output buffer U12, then the DAC U11. The output buffer sits between U10 and U11, next to the DAC's INV, VOUT and RFB pins at the top of U11, so that its input and feedback tracks stay short. The tuning filter lies below the OCXO's tuning pin. The clock buffer and the two SMA connectors are on the left edge. J8 sits on the bottom edge at the right, with its pins over the edge, so that the cable leaves the board's bottom edge in the board's plane. U13 lies between J8 and the DAC, with side 2 towards the DAC, and its tracks to the DAC's SPI pins are 15 to 17 mm long.

Each board also needs a 12 V adapter of at least 2 A with a floating output and a 5.5 × 2.1 mm plug with the center pin positive, which fits J1, a 2×6 Pmod extension cable (see The SPI input from the RFSoC 4x2) and M3 screws for the base plate. Where to buy the OCXO, and its lead time, is not yet known. The low parts go on first and the OCXO last. J8 is soldered with its plastic body at the board's bottom edge and its pins over the edge. Each SMA connector clips over the board edge and is soldered at five pads, the center pin on the top, and two ground legs on the top and two on the bottom of the board. The ground pads join the ground fill and, through vias, the ground planes, which draw the heat away, so they need a large tip at 350 to 380 °C.

## What the board does not do

It makes no 1PPS. The FS725's 1PPS is node B's PPS input today, from which the PL counts seconds, so without the FS725 that input has to come from elsewhere, which is decided in the PL, not on this board.

## Simulations

Both run in LTspice XVII from `sim/run_sims.py` (`python hw/ocxo_board/sim/run_sims.py`), which writes `sim/sim_results.json` and the figure, `sim/sim_results.png`. They were last run on 2026-11-20 for this board.

![LTspice results](sim/sim_results.png)

1. **The tuning voltage path** (`sim/efc_*.cir`, named after EFC, electronic frequency control, another name for the OCXO's tuning input Vcon). It uses LTspice's own models of the LTC6655-5 reference and the AD8675 output buffer. The AD5791 is its output voltage behind the 3.4 kΩ of its resistor ladder, whose thermal noise is the DAC's own noise of 7.5 nV/√Hz, and the reference buffers are left out. After the AD8675 come 5 pF for DAC_OUT's 48 mm of track (about 1 pF per cm over the ground plane, inferred), the 100 Ω, the 1.1 µF, the BAV199 clamp and 50 kΩ to 2.5 V for the OCXO's input.
   - Panel (a) shows the change at Vcon and at the AD8675's output after the DAC steps by 1 mV. Vcon reaches 99 % after 0.51 ms, without overshoot, far inside the 0.02 s between corrections, while the AD8675's output follows at once.
   - Panel (b) shows the gain from the DAC's output to Vcon and to the AD8675's output against frequency. The AD8675's output peaks by 0.09 dB at most, so the 100 Ω keeps the capacitors from making it unstable. The corner of the low-pass comes out at 1.44 kHz.
   - Panel (c) shows the noise density at Vcon with the DAC at mid scale. From 0.1 to 10 Hz it adds up to 0.18 µV rms, about 1×10⁻¹⁴ at the smallest tuning slope and a hundred times below 1×10⁻¹². From 10 Hz to 1 kHz it is 1.3 µV rms, mostly the reference's noise at half scale (inferred from the shape of the spectrum).
   - The models have no temperature drift, so the drift is the data sheet figure in The DAC section (reference 5 µV per °C at mid scale).
2. **The 10 MHz output** (`sim/clock_edge.cir`). The buffer is an edge of 0.7, 1.5 or 3 ns behind 20 Ω, the typical output resistance of an LVC output at 3.3 V (an assumption, not the part's IBIS model). It drives 33 Ω, 100 nF and 1 m of 50 Ω cable into either the 4x2's input (100 nF, a 3 dB pi attenuator, 50 Ω) or a scope input of 1 MΩ and 15 pF.
   - Panel (d) shows the rising edge at the LMK04828 behind the 4x2's input. The swing is 1.14 V peak to peak and the 20 to 80 % edge is 1.62, 0.76 and 0.38 V/ns for the three edge times. The LMK04828 needs 0.15 V/ns.
   - Panel (e) shows the voltage at the high-impedance scope input. The 100 nF in series blocks DC, so in steady operation the swing of 3.3 V centres on 0 V, and the panel shows it less its mean, since the 30 µs simulation is far shorter than the 0.1 s the 100 nF takes to charge through 1 MΩ.
   - Panel (f) shows the buffer's output current for the 1.5 ns edge. It peaks at 18 to 22 mA into the 4x2 for the three edge times and at 34 to 42 mA into the high-impedance scope input, while charging the cable, which is why J7 must end in 50 Ω.

## To check before ordering

- **The OCXO's footprint.** Compare it with the outline drawing on page 4 of the data sheet, best by laying a real OCXO on a 1:1 print of the board.
- **The connectors' footprints.** Check that the barrel jack DC-005-A200 matches KiCad's BarrelJack_Horizontal footprint and that its pin diameter fits the adapter's plug, that the Amphenol 132289 drawing matches the SMA footprint, and that the right-angle 2×6 header that will be bought matches J8's footprint.
- **U13's part.** Check that the BOM's U13 is the ISO7741, whose outputs stay high while side 1 has no power, and not the ISO7741F.
- **C11.** Check from the Samsung CL32A476MOJNNNE's DC-bias curve that it keeps at least 25 µF at 5 V, which U2's output needs.
- **The LT3471's feedback.** Check R32 to R35, copied from the CN0531, against the LT3471 data sheet, so that the outputs come out at ±8 V.
- **The reference's package.** Compare the LTC6655's thermal hysteresis and long-term drift in the MSOP with those in the LS8 package, and find whether the LS8 can be bought and placed, in which case it replaces the MSOP.
- **The OCXO's tuning input.** Check in the OCXO's data sheet that Vcon may go a diode's drop beyond its ground and its supply, as the clamp allows.
- **The OCXO's source.** Find where to buy the NI-10M-3660 and its lead time.
- **A second look at the layout.** Ask someone who has laid out precision analog boards to look at it, above all the buck converter, U2, the reference with its force and sense tracks, the ground under the AD5791 and the tuning voltage path.

## To check on the bench

- **The Pmod mapping.** Before the board arrives, run P14's test signal with the cable unplugged and see on the scope which of the 4x2's J1 pins carry SDIN, SCLK and SYNC, and with a jumper which pin is SDO's. A wrong mapping is fixed in the pin constraints. Check too that the cable is wired pin to pin, with a male plug at the 4x2 and a female socket at J8, that its plug fits at PMOD0's end of the 4x2's J1 beside the RS-232 module at the other end, and, once the board is connected, that SCLK's falling edge at U13 does not ring back across U13's input thresholds.
- **The OCXO's response.** Measure how fast the OCXO's frequency follows its tuning voltage, its lag, which sets what the steering can reach (see What it should give against the FS725), and the unit's frequency offset at 2.5 V and its tuning slope, since the data sheet gives only limits.
- **U3's load.** Measure U3's current with the LT3471 running, expected about 0.1 A (see The supplies) against U3's rating of 0.5 A.
- **The 10 MHz's jitter.** Measure the 10 MHz's jitter or phase noise against a clean reference, such as the FS725's 10 MHz, with the DAC section running and with it stopped. Shorting C40 and C41 holds the LT3471's soft-start pins low, which stops it (inferred from the CN0531's circuit). The parts of the LT3471's boost converter lie 23 to 33 mm from the clock buffer U5, C42 nearest and U6 and L2 farthest, and the measurement shows whether its 1.2 MHz switching reaches the clock.

## How the files are made

The scripts run in this order from the repository root. Those that call pcbnew run with KiCad's own Python (`C:/Users/FHYQ-Dong/scoop/apps/kicad/current/bin/python.exe` on the development machine), the others with any Python that has numpy and matplotlib.

1. **Footprints.** `python hw/ocxo_board/gen/footprints.py` writes `ocxo_board.pretty` (the OCXO's footprint and J8's) and `ocxo_board.3dshapes` (the OCXO's box model).
2. **Schematic.** `python hw/ocxo_board/gen/gen_sch.py` writes `ocxo_board.kicad_sch` and the symbol library, with the parts other than the DAC section and the SPI input taken from `gen/base_sch.py`, and the part numbers from `gen/parts.py` and the list in `gen_sch.py`. KiCad's `kicad-cli sch erc` checks it and `kicad-cli sch export pdf -o hw/ocxo_board/ocxo_board.pdf hw/ocxo_board/ocxo_board.kicad_sch` writes the PDF.
3. **Layout.** With KiCad's Python, `hw/ocxo_board/gen/gen_pcb.py` places the parts and routes the board once. Freerouting's single pass comes out differently from run to run, so the board in the repository was kept by `python hw/ocxo_board/gen/best_of.py hw/ocxo_board ocxo_board 3`, which runs gen_pcb.py up to three times and keeps the run with the fewest DRC errors and unconnected items. A new routing has to pass the checks of this section and JLCPCB's placement preview again. After a change of a part's value or part number alone, `hw/ocxo_board/gen/sync_fields.py hw/ocxo_board ocxo_board` (KiCad's Python) copies the fields onto the board without routing again.
4. **Ground figure.** `hw/ocxo_board/gen/ground_layers.py dump hw/ocxo_board/ocxo_board.kicad_pcb hw/ocxo_board/build/ground_layers.json` (KiCad's Python) and then `python hw/ocxo_board/gen/ground_layers.py plot hw/ocxo_board/build/ground_layers.json hw/ocxo_board/fab/ground_layers.png U1 U2 X1 U6 U7 U8 U9 U10 U11 U12 U13 J8` (with `--zh` and `ground_layers_zh.png` for the Chinese one).
5. **Fabrication files.** `python hw/ocxo_board/gen/fab.py` writes `fab/`.
